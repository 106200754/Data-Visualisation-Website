The road-crash injuries behind the headlines

Follow how hospitalisations from road crashes changed between 2011 and 2021, who was affected most, and how the counts differ across states and territories.

About this dashboard

Who is carrying the rise in road-crash hospitalisations?

Road-crash deaths get most of the attention, but far more people are admitted to hospital with injuries. This dashboard follows hospitalised road-crash injuries in Australia from 2011 to 2021, and looks at what changed for First Nations peoples and for selected road-user and age groups.

How to explore the story

  1. 01
    Start with the national picture

    Hospitalisations rose from 34,033 in 2011 to 39,505 in 2021 (+16%), with a dip in 2020.

  2. 02
    See who changed the most

    First Nations hospitalisations rose by about 90%. Among the selected groups, cyclists and people aged 65–74 rose fastest.

  3. 03
    Check the states, then the limits

    Victoria, Queensland and NSW have the highest 2021 counts, but these are raw counts, not rates adjusted for population.

DataSources, measures & limits

About the data

What the figures on this dashboard come from, what they cover, and how far they can be pushed.

01

Source

BITRE Hospitalised Injury Data for road crashes, built from the AIHW National Hospital Morbidity Database. Our charts use annual tables we prepared from the BITRE files in KNIME.

02

Coverage

Hospital admissions for road-crash injuries from 2011 to 2021: national and First Nations yearly totals, the change in selected road-user and age groups, and state and territory counts for 2021.

03

Measure

Each figure is a count of hospitalisations, meaning admissions, not people. One person admitted twice in a year can appear twice. The group chart shows percentage change from 2011 to 2021.

How to read the figures

These are recorded counts, not rates. They are not adjusted for population size or how much people travel, so a larger count does not mean a higher risk. Read the charts as a record of what was admitted to hospital, not as a ranking of safety.

Suppressed values: “n.p.” means not published. Small numbers are withheld for confidentiality or quality reasons. It is not the same as zero.

Questions & answers

Understanding the data

What does a hospitalisation mean here?

An admission to hospital with an injury from a road crash. Minor injuries treated without admission are not included, and neither are deaths at the scene.

Can every year be compared directly?

Mostly, but with care. Reporting practices can change over time, and 2020 shows a dip that we do not attribute to any single cause. Check the BITRE data dictionary for notes on individual jurisdictions before drawing firm conclusions.

Why do the totals differ between charts?

Some records have an unknown category (for example First Nations status or state) and some cells are suppressed, so tables can add up slightly differently. For example, the 2021 state counts add up to 39,035, while the national total for 2021 is 39,505.

What can counts tell us about risk?

Very little on their own. First Nations peoples are a much smaller share of the population than non-Indigenous Australians, and states differ greatly in size, so comparing raw counts can mislead. A fair comparison needs rates per head of population, which this dashboard does not calculate.
